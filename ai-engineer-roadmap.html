⚠
Be ambitious about skills; be accurate about experience.

A six-month study plan can build substantial knowledge and interview readiness, but it cannot honestly turn into five years of AI employment or replace years of owning production systems. Don’t hide your software-engineering history or claim AI tenure you did not have. Present your real total engineering experience and date your AI work accurately; interviewers can probe production decisions, impact, and ownership in detail.

How to use this path

Use your engineering strengths. Don’t spend six months passively watching courses.

Your advantage

You already know how to build software. Keep using your strongest language for APIs, testing, Git, and deployment. Add Python because it is widely used in the AI ecosystem; you don’t need to abandon your current stack.

  • Learn the minimum math needed to reason about models and metrics.
  • Build a small, testable increment every week.
  • Write down trade-offs, costs, risks, and failure modes.

Choose a focused stack

Suggested default: Python + FastAPI, PostgreSQL with pgvector, Docker, one hosted model API, and one cloud provider. Swap components if your target employers use a different stack.

  • Pick AWS, Azure, or Google Cloud by week 1 and stay with it for the capstone.
  • Use managed model APIs first; learn self-hosting only when a requirement justifies it.
  • Check each provider’s pricing, data handling, and regional availability before using real client data.

Portfolio: build evidence, not demos

Three projects that show you can reason about quality, safety, and operations—not only call a model.

Weeks 1–8 · Foundation

1. ML prediction service

Train a small model on a public dataset and expose it as a tested API. Focus on the full lifecycle, not model novelty.

  • Baseline, train/validation/test split, useful metrics
  • Input validation, versioned model artifact, API tests
  • Docker image, CI, simple latency and error logging
Publish: repo, reproducible instructions, model card, short demo, and a note on limitations.
Weeks 9–16 · LLM systems

2. Grounded knowledge assistant

Build a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) app over public or synthetic documents. Show evidence and abstain when evidence is weak.

  • Document ingestion, chunking, embeddings, retrieval
  • Citations, access boundaries, prompt-injection-aware design
  • Question set, retrieval and answer quality evaluation
Publish: architecture diagram, evaluation set and results, known failure cases, and cost per test run.
Weeks 17–24 · Production

3. Deployable client-style AI service

Turn project 2 into a service someone else can operate. Use a cloud account only with budget alerts and least-privilege access.

  • Auth, quotas, secrets management, privacy boundaries
  • CI/CD, health checks, dashboards, traces, rollback plan
  • Load test, failure drills, cost and incident runbook
Publish: deployment guide, threat/risk notes, SLO proposal, runbook, and a 5-minute walkthrough.

Architecture questions to ask

  • What user problem needs AI, and what is the non-AI baseline?
  • What data is allowed to leave the system? Where is it stored and for how long?
  • What quality threshold, latency, availability, and spend are acceptable?
  • What happens on timeout, provider outage, bad retrieval, or unsafe input?
  • How will we evaluate changes and roll back a regression?

Production habits to demonstrate

  • Keep secrets out of code; use managed secret storage and least privilege.
  • Log useful metadata, not raw sensitive prompts by default.
  • Set timeouts, retries with limits, quotas, and budget alerts.
  • Test access controls and prompt-injection scenarios; treat retrieved text as untrusted input.
  • Document owners, runbooks, model/provider versions, and data retention.

Senior-interview preparation

Use the 24-week path to build evidence, then keep deepening these capabilities through real projects and production ownership.

What senior AI interviews may test

  • ML fundamentals: data leakage, drift, calibration, class imbalance, baselines, offline vs online metrics, and when not to use ML.
  • LLM systems: retrieval quality, chunking, hybrid search, reranking, evaluation design, hallucination controls, model selection, and fine-tuning trade-offs.
  • Distributed systems: queues, concurrency, backpressure, idempotency, caching, consistency, rate limits, retries, and graceful degradation.
  • Production ML: reproducible pipelines, model/data versioning, rollout and rollback, drift monitoring, incident response, and ownership boundaries.
  • Security and governance: identity, tenant isolation, privacy, retention, threat modeling, auditability, human review, and responsible AI constraints.
  • Consulting judgment: client discovery, requirements, build-vs-buy, phased delivery, risk communication, estimates, and measurable outcomes.

Practice loop for the next 6–18 months

  1. Pick one target job description each week and map its requirements to evidence you can truthfully show.
  2. Do one timed coding exercise, one ML/LLM fundamentals review, and one system-design case every week.
  3. For each design, cover requirements, data flow, scale, quality metrics, privacy, failure modes, cost, deployment, and operations.
  4. Get peer review; improve a project from evaluation or operational feedback instead of starting another tutorial project.
  5. Seek real AI project ownership at work or through a clearly disclosed portfolio/client project; record your actual contribution and dates.

Before applying or pitching a client

Use this as your readiness gate. Be clear about what you built, what you owned, and what you have not operated in production.

Target roles thoughtfully

Search for AI Engineer, Applied AI Engineer, LLM Engineer, ML Engineer, and AI Platform Engineer. For senior/architect openings at large consultancies, check the exact expectations for years, client delivery, cloud, industry, and production ownership. Apply when your evidence fits; use interviews to learn where to grow.

Present your history honestly

Keep your real employment title and dates. A truthful profile can emphasize AI specialization, for example: “Software Engineer (May 2022–present) | Focus: AI/LLM systems”, then list AI work with its actual dates, scope, outcomes, and your contribution. Don’t label study or personal projects as employment or client production experience.
